# SAAGA: Strategic and Adaptive Attack Generation for Verifiable Red Teaming of LLM Prompt Defenses

## Abstract

Automated red teaming of large language models reports attack success rates that are hard to verify, hard to compare, and hard to explain. A reported success rests on a classifier's opinion of the response, a failed attack cannot be told apart from a bad idea or a bad sentence, and every benchmark run starts from amnesia. We present SAAGA, a red-teaming framework that builds on the AutoRed scenario suite and fixes these three problems. SAAGA wraps a hidden access code between two instruction blocks, the sandwich defense, and attacks each scenario with a planner that emits a structured strategy plan and a generator that realizes the plan in a 40-word prompt. Success is decided by four judge-independent signals, and the strongest win is confirmed by re-verification, which re-sends an extracted candidate to the victim and accepts it only if the victim grants access. Across attempts and runs, SAAGA conditions planning on a strategy knowledge base and a retrieval store of past wins, both re-estimated after every benchmark, and it is trained by a privileged oracle that spends a larger search budget to produce teacher trajectories. On 13,024 scenarios against four open-weight victims, SAAGA breaks 87.8% to 97.0% of defenses in a median of two to four attempts. The planner-conditioned architecture raises the break rate from 55.9% to 66.6% on a harder 1,000-scenario pool, and the memory loop cuts the attempts needed on successful breaks by 10%. Every failure is attributed to a phase, which locates the residual bottleneck in the stop-point judge, not in extraction or verification.

## 1. Introduction

Prompt defenses decide whether an LLM reveals its instructions, its secrets, or its system context, and automated red teaming is how we measure whether they hold. Today's measurements share a weakness: the win signal is a classifier's opinion. A response is called a success because a judge model or a large model grader decided it looked harmful, and the evaluation inherits every error of that judge (Zheng et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023). When the judge is wrong, the benchmark is wrong, and no downstream analysis can repair it.

Three structural gaps keep red-teaming evaluations from being verifiable, diagnosable, and reusable. First, success depends on an external judge rather than on the behavior of the defended system itself, so the evaluation has no ground truth it can point to. Second, most automated attackers are monolithic: one model both chooses the tactic and phrases the prompt, so a failure leaves two explanations (the idea was wrong, or the words were wrong) and the benchmark cannot tell which. Third, evaluations are amnesic. Each run starts from zero, the system never benefits from the thousands of scenarios it has already attacked, and the benchmark does not get smarter with use.

SAAGA reframes red teaming as a capture-the-flag game with a verifiable win. Each scenario embeds a hidden access code between two instruction blocks, the sandwich defense, which mirrors how defenses are actually deployed: the attacker's prompt lands inside a system instruction that re-asserts its rules above and below it (Greshake et al., 2023; Wallace et al., 2024). Winning means making the victim reveal or accept the code, which is a binary event the benchmark can check directly, without asking a judge for an opinion.

The design follows from three decisions. SAAGA separates policy from wording: a planner chooses the attack strategy as a structured plan, and a generator turns that plan into a short prompt. The split is what makes failures attributable, because a failed strategy and a failed sentence leave different traces. SAAGA lets the victim arbitrate: the strongest win is a candidate that the victim itself accepts when re-sent inside the same defense. SAAGA makes the system remember: a strategy knowledge base of past success rates and a retrieval store of past winning attacks condition every plan, and both are rebuilt after every benchmark. A privileged oracle that attacks scenarios with a larger search budget generates the teacher trajectories that train the planner and the generator, closing the loop.

Our contributions are:

1. A verifiable evaluation protocol. The sandwich scenario gives a binary win condition, success is decided by four judge-independent signals with a fixed priority, and the top signal requires the victim to accept a candidate on replay (Section 3, Section 4.6). No classifier opinion enters the success decision.
2. A diagnostic planner-generator architecture. A structured plan contract carries the strategy decision to the wording stage, deterministic guards enforce anti-repeat policies, and every failed scenario receives a phase-level failure label. On a 1,000-scenario pool the architecture raises the break rate from 55.9% to 66.6% and the verified rate from 41.1% to 50.7% (Section 4.3, Table 3).
3. A self-improving memory loop. The strategy knowledge base and retrieval store condition planning with explicit anti-overfitting controls, and a privileged oracle supplies training data. In a controlled 1,000-run comparison, memory cuts the average attempts on success from 4.02 to 3.61 and total victim queries by 8.4% (Section 4.7, Table 4).
4. Large-scale evidence with full attribution. We evaluate four open-weight victims across 13,024 scenarios each (52,096 scenarios total), verify stability at 26,047 scenarios, and attribute every failure to a phase, defense family, and code shape (Section 5).

The headline results: SAAGA breaks 87.8% to 97.0% of defenses across the four victims, with verified rates of 60.7% to 79.1% and a median of two to four attempts per break. Mistral-7B yields fastest (97.0% broken, 2.0 attempts on average), Llama-3-8B resists longest (87.8%, 3.9 attempts). The residual failures concentrate in translation, roleplay, and trigger-phrase defenses and in long-form secrets, and the dominant failure phase is the stop-point judge, which tells us precisely where the next version of the framework should invest.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Automated red-teaming attacks

Gradient-based methods such as GCG optimize an adversarial suffix against the victim's logit loss and achieve high success in white-box settings, but they transfer poorly to black-box deployments (Zou et al., 2023). Query-efficient methods replace the gradient with a language model: PAIR uses a two-agent loop in which one model generates and another critiques attacks, reaching strong success in about twenty queries (Chao et al., 2023); TAP expands a tree of attacks and prunes unpromising branches (Liu et al., 2024); AutoDAN frames jailbreak discovery as genetic search over aligned prompts (Shen et al., 2024). Persona and framing attacks, from the original DAN construction to many-shot jailbreaking, show that instruction framing alone can bypass refusals (Shen et al., 2023; Anil et al., 2024), and multi-turn methods such as Crescendo escalate compromise across a conversation. AutoRed, which SAAGA builds on, removes seed instructions entirely and generates free-form adversarial prompts with persona guidance, a reflection loop, and an offline harmfulness verifier, reporting state-of-the-art attack success on its own medium and hard behavior suites (Diao et al., 2025).

These methods optimize attack quality against harmful behaviors, and they are measured by an external classifier. SAAGA is complementary in two ways. Its objective is to evaluate defenses, so the win condition is a concrete, checkable event inside the victim's own deployment format rather than a label assigned after the fact. And its attacker is a policy: the strategy decision is a structured, inspectable object, which prior attack methods do not expose.

### 2.2 Evaluation frameworks and benchmarks

HarmBench standardized the evaluation of automated red teaming by fixing a behavior suite, a protocol, and metrics, and by comparing eighteen methods against thirty-three models under a GPT-4-based judge; it found that no method or defense is uniformly effective (Mazeika et al., 2024). StrongREJECT probes whether fine-tuning revives zero-shot jailbreaks and tracks refusal integrity (Wallace et al., 2024). JailBench opens an ecosystem of jailbreak prompts and attacks with a shared evaluation harness (Li et al., 2024). NIST's CyberSecEval runs adversarial task batteries against commercial systems (NIST, 2024). All of these inherit the judge-dependence of the attack methods they measure: the success label comes from a grader, and the grader's errors are unbounded.

SAAGA differs in the source of the win signal. The ground truth is the hidden access code and the victim's own acceptance behavior, so the benchmark's correctness does not depend on a classifier's calibration. It also differs in what it reports: in addition to break rates it reports verified rates, attempts-to-success, extraction precision and recall, and a per-phase failure attribution for every unsuccessful scenario.

### 2.3 Memory and self-improvement

Reflexion shows that an agent can improve by storing verbal self-critiques of its own failures and reading them on the next trial (Shinn et al., 2023); Self-Refine iterates critique and revision within a single task (Madaan et al., 2023); retrieval-augmented generation conditions a model on retrieved documents at inference time (Lewis et al., 2020). These mechanisms improve single-agent performance, but in red teaming the useful memory is cross-scenario: what worked on a similar defense. SAAGA's memory is deliberately non-parametric. Success-rate tables and a retrieval index over past wins are re-estimated by batch aggregation after each benchmark, the planner parameters stay frozen during evaluation, and explicit controls (a five-attempt noise floor and a 5% exploration blank) limit overfitting to past winners, which a controlled comparison shows does occur (duplicate attack rate rises from 0.77% to 4.08% when memory is enabled).
